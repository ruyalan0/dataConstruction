name: pour-liquid-into-cup
description: Grasp the broad bottle body, choose a stable tilt axis and cup approach side from the grasp, and pour at the rim or shallowly inside; observe drainage and restore upright.
---

Before the final return upright, establish an empty-bottle assessment through direct inspection or the sufficient indirect drainage evidence described below. Reserve simulation time for drainage, observation and the upright return after the grasp and transfer. The public completion condition requires enough liquid in the cup and an upright bottle: emptying the bottle is the pouring strategy, and does not establish success if liquid was spilled. Check native completion after restoring the bottle.

Identify the bottle outlet and the cup's interior from head and wrist RGB. Choose a grasp that leaves the outlet and its future flow path observable. After a retention lift, establish a view of the cup interior and the area immediately outside its rim before pouring. The empty opposite hand can supply an oblique observation view from clear space when the carrying gripper occludes the landing point. A stream overlapping the cup in one image may pass in front of or behind it.

Identify the base, broad body, shoulder, narrow neck and mouth before closing. Grasp the broad middle-to-lower bottle body below the shoulder, with opposing finger pads across its thick section and enough clearance above the table and base. Do not grasp the narrow neck, mouth or shoulder taper. Open enough to clear the body, check pad placement from paired views, close separately and verify a short upright retention lift. If the body cannot be reached or cleared, revise the approach or use supported repositioning or handoff, then grasp the broad body again; a neck grasp is not a fallback. Verify a small rotation in clear space before moving to the cup.

The task uses its original linear interpolation for approach, mouth contact or insertion, incremental tilt, position compensation and upright recovery. Linear references limit velocity without enforcing an acceleration bound. Choose short observed joint goals near the cup; interpolation alone does not establish mouth alignment, contact or retention. Do not add motion_stage, duration or speed fields to control_arms.

Choose the pouring tilt axis from the achieved grasp before choosing which side of the cup to approach. Distinguish the fingers' extension direction, the closing line between opposing pads and the bottle's base-to-mouth axis. Prefer a side-over tilt about an axis parallel to finger extension (X5 wrist local +X, usually wrist roll), so the retained bottle tilts with the whole gripper. An axis near or along one finger describes the tilt direction; it does not require that finger to remain fixed in world space. Rotation about the bottle's own long axis does not establish pouring tilt. Use a small non-flowing rotation in clear space to verify the actual base and mouth move with the pads without appreciable relative pivoting or sliding. Another tilt axis needs its own successful test; a roll test does not validate subsequent pitch.

Use the verified tilt direction to choose the non-flowing staging side and bottle-to-cup alignment. If a test moves the mouth away from the cup, evaluate the opposite rotation sign with a clear sweep or reposition the held bottle around the cup so the tested tilt carries the mouth inward and raises the base. Do not replace a stable tilt with unverified pitch merely because the cup is ahead of the wrist. If the grasp cannot support a clear pouring-and-return sweep, set the bottle on visible support and revise the broad-body grasp or handoff, then test again. Remember the chosen axis, sign and approach side, and recheck their actual effect after any grasp or wrist-orientation change.

A translation test lift does not establish control during tilt. Compare the body and outlet against the pads during the intended pouring rotation. If the opposing contacts stay nearly fixed while the wrist turns and the bottle pivots between them, or the bottle remains upright despite wrist tilt, stop extending that rotation. Stable joints and unchanged finger opening do not establish retention. Assess the actual outlet direction, reduce flow if needed, and recover through clear space to supported repositioning or regrasp. Confirm rotational control again before continuing; do not reuse a bottle-to-wrist relationship that has changed.

With the bottle not flowing, approach the cup slowly and let the held bottle settle. Keep the cup upright and supported on the table. Plan the full bottle, base, gripper and wrist sweep for tilt and recovery. Bring the bottle mouth into light contact with the cup rim, with the outlet directed into the interior, before sustaining flow. A mouth hovering above the cup is only an approach waypoint. If the mouth fits through the opening with clearance for the intended rotations and withdrawal, it may instead enter shallowly below the rim, with its outlet inside the cup. Judge fit and depth from head and wrist views; keep the mouth clear of the bottom and avoid wedging the neck against opposing walls. Use observed geometry rather than a fixed insertion depth. The outlet's vertical projection into the cup is only an initial reference; a wrist or finger center above the cup does not establish mouth contact or insertion.

Establish contact or shallow insertion with small observed goals. Apply small orientation changes about the verified tilt direction and coordinate translation using the actual mouth location, so the mouth stays lightly at the inner rim or inside the opening while the body tilts. The tilt axis sets the orientation change; translation compensates the mouth arc instead of rigidly fixing a finger or using the rim as a pivot. Carry the bottle's weight with the gripper; do not press, drag, lever or support the bottle on the cup. The rest of the bottle and gripper must clear the rim and handle. If the cup moves, tilts or the mouth binds, pause further tilt, reduce flow and unload contact, then revise the approach. Do not force a larger rotation while the mouth is constrained. If the intended tilt cannot preserve a clear sweep, stop flow and reposition before continuing.

Liquid has inertia: its velocity at release combines flow relative to the bottle with the outlet's own motion. Translating or rotating the bottle can therefore sweep the stream sideways even when its final pose looks aligned. A wrist rotation also moves the outlet along an arc; use the observed bottle-to-gripper relationship to make small coordinated joint changes that keep the outlet near the same rim contact point or shallow position inside the opening. Abrupt acceleration, reversal or repeated tilt oscillation can create a surge or slosh. Aim for a nearly stationary outlet and a narrow, steady stream directed inside the cup.

Use successive head and wrist RGB observations to track the actual exit direction, airborne path and cup entry. Liquid already airborne continues along its old path after the bottle moves, while liquid still in the neck may keep flowing after tilt is reduced. Evaluate a correction only after newly emitted liquid is visible; chasing old drops with another correction can cause overshoot. Waiting for an API response or reviewing an old frame does not advance simulated drainage: repeat the current joint goal to obtain a new settling observation at fixed pose. Keep hidden liquid positions and velocities out of this judgment.

Remember the last observed non-flowing orientation. Approach first flow with small intermediate joint goals, keeping the mouth lightly at the rim or shallowly inside the opening; shorten each observation interval near onset. After an increment, repeat the achieved joint goal for another settling observation and inspect the outlet, cup interior and surrounding table. Flow can start after a delay at fixed tilt. Keep the achieved angle while waiting for this response instead of increasing tilt because the first frame has no stream. Establish a small flow entering the cup before increasing drainage. If the stream already enters cleanly, preserve that pose while it drains; a larger tilt is not automatically better.

If the new stream escapes across the outer rim or fresh spill appears, promptly reduce tilt toward the remembered non-flowing orientation in a short controlled motion while keeping the outlet directed into the cup. Avoid a fast withdrawal or a broad rotation that sends the remaining neck flow across the table. Hold there until fresh outflow stops and residual drops have landed. Unload rim contact or clear a shallow insertion before translating sideways; do not drag the mouth across the rim. Correct the observed miss direction at fixed tilt, re-establish light contact or shallow insertion, then observe low flow before sustaining it. Change one cause at a time—outlet position or tilt—so the observed response can guide the next action; preserve cup stability throughout.

When successive observations show cup entry and no growing spill, maintain the pouring pose for short observed intervals and inspect the bottle for remaining liquid. If flow dwindles with liquid remaining, add only a small tilt increment while compensating the outlet arc, then hold and recheck entry. Drain a residual pool in the body, shoulder or neck the same way, without shaking the bottle. Liquid already visible in the cup or elapsed pouring time does not establish an empty bottle. Evaluate stopped flow together with the actual outlet orientation using the criteria below.

When the contents are visible, inspect the liquid-holding body, shoulder and neck for residual pools, together with the end of sustained flow at a draining orientation. Obtain another permitted RGB view when the gripper or bottle angle hides the relevant region. A visible residual pool requires further draining even when no stream is currently visible.

For an opaque bottle, a visibly vertically downward outlet with no outflow across fresh settled observations supports an indirect empty-bottle assessment. This steeper draining pose already exceeds the 45-degree checkpoint below; no further downward tilt is needed. Assess the actual bottle body and outlet, accounting for rotation within the grasp; wrist angle alone does not establish the outlet direction. An occluded outlet or uncertain orientation leaves the assessment unresolved. Still use the observed trial return described below before a larger upright recovery.

Shallow insertion can hide the outlet. An absent external stream from an occluded mouth does not establish emptying. Obtain a clear permitted view, or after apparent flow has stopped, carefully unload contact and lift the mouth just clear of the rim while preserving a draining orientation and directing any renewed flow into the cup. Hold for a fresh observation and apply the same visible-outlet drainage criteria. If flow resumes, re-establish the observed pouring contact or shallow insertion when feasible and continue draining.

Use about 45 degrees below the horizontal tabletop as the first residual-drainage checkpoint: the bottle base is above the mouth, and its base-to-mouth axis points downward at about 45 degrees to the tabletop (about 135 degrees of tilt from the initial upright bottle). Judge the achieved bottle axis, not a wrist-joint target. Once the established stream dwindles or stops at a shallower pose, increase the actual downward tilt in small steps of about 5-10 degrees along the verified axis, compensating the mouth arc with translation and checking grip retention, cup stability and the full sweep. Keep the outlet at the inner rim or shallowly inside the cup so renewed flow is caught. Do not infer emptying merely from stopped flow at a shallower angle plus a small tilt check.

At approximately 45 degrees, hold the achieved joint goal for at least two successive fresh settled observations with the outlet and landing region visible. If flow continues, keep that pose and drain; reaching the checkpoint alone does not establish an empty bottle. If flow stops across these checks and no visible residual pool remains, record a provisional indirect assessment and perform the trial return below. If residual liquid or renewed outflow calls for more drainage, resume the last clear pouring pose; a further small tilt toward about 60 degrees is an option only when needed and the sweep remains clear. Do not force 60 degrees when the 45-degree checks and trial return show no renewed flow. These angles are experimental drainage checkpoints, not evaluator thresholds or guarantees of emptying. If the checkpoint cannot be reached with retention and clearance, keep the assessment unresolved and recover within the remaining simulation budget.

Record whether the assessment is direct, indirect or unresolved, with the supporting same-episode observations. For the vertically downward outlet criterion, explicitly state: "Judged empty indirectly: the visible bottle outlet was vertically downward with no liquid flowing out; the contents were not directly inspected." For the 45-degree route, record the achieved bottle angle, fresh no-flow checks and trial-return response, and label the conclusion indirect. If flow resumes, continue draining and reassess. If the outlet or effective draining pose remains uncertain, obtain a permitted view or keep the assessment unresolved. Do not read hidden liquid volume or substitute an unobserved quantity for this evidence.

Before a larger upright recovery, first test a small return of about 5-10 degrees along the verified reverse tilt axis, then hold for a fresh settled observation of the visible outlet and cup entry. Coordinate translation so the outlet stays in the cup's catchment area and close to the rim; unload contact or retract only just enough to clear the rim if the small test requires it. If fresh liquid appears, invalidate the empty assessment, pause the return and continue catching and draining it before reassessing. Do not combine the first trial return with a large lift or sideways withdrawal.

After direct confirmation or sufficient indirect drainage evidence and a trial return without renewed flow, unload rim contact or retract the shallowly inserted mouth just clear of the rim before any large upright rotation. Use short observed goals, keeping the outlet directed into the cup for residual drops and checking that neither the rim nor the bottle base blocks the sweep. Once clear, reverse the verified tilt in observed increments with coordinated translation over the cup, monitoring fresh outflow, bottle-to-pad retention and the base sweep. Renewed outflow during any increment requires pausing and catching it before further recovery. Repeat the achieved joint goal for another observation when upright, then withdraw through clear space and check native completion. Include the indirect basis when reporting that assessment. If completion remains false, report the observed state and remaining uncertainty rather than declaring success. Repeated near-inversions cannot recover liquid already lost on the table.
