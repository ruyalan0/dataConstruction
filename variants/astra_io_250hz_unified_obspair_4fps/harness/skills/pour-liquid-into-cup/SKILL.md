---
name: pour-liquid-into-cup
description: Pour into a cup with a stable low outlet, gradual tilt and observation of liquid inertia; verify sustained cup entry and drainage before restoring the bottle upright.
---

Before the final return upright, establish an empty-bottle assessment through direct inspection or the sufficient indirect drainage evidence described below. Reserve native steps for drainage, observation and the upright return after the grasp and transfer. The public completion condition requires enough liquid in the cup and an upright bottle: emptying the bottle is the pouring strategy, and does not establish success if liquid was spilled. Check native completion after restoring the bottle.

Identify the bottle outlet and the cup's interior from head and wrist RGB. Choose a grasp that leaves the outlet and its future flow path observable. After a retention lift, establish a view of the cup interior and the area immediately outside its rim before pouring. The empty opposite hand can supply an oblique observation view from clear space when the carrying gripper occludes the landing point. A stream overlapping the cup in one image may pass in front of or behind it.

A translation test lift does not establish control of the bottle's orientation during tilt. Compare the body and outlet against the finger pads during the first small rotations. Stable joint readings, a captured neck or unchanged finger opening can coexist with the bottle pivoting inside the grasp. If this occurs, assess the actual outlet direction before adding tilt, recover a visibly supported pose through a clear sweep and choose a grasp that resists the required rotation. Confirm its behavior again before continuing; do not plan the pouring arc as if the previous bottle-to-wrist relationship still held.

With the bottle not flowing, approach the cup slowly and let the held bottle settle before tilting. Keep the cup supported on the table. Plan a bottle and gripper sweep that clears its rim and handle. Position the outlet low above the opening, with visible clearance through the intended tilt arc, so the stream has little distance to travel. Judge clearance from head and wrist views. The outlet's vertical projection into the cup is only an initial reference; a wrist or finger center above the cup does not establish outlet alignment.

Liquid has inertia: its velocity at release combines flow relative to the bottle with the outlet's own motion. Translating or rotating the bottle can therefore sweep the stream sideways even when its final pose looks aligned. A wrist rotation also moves the outlet along an arc; use the observed bottle-to-gripper relationship to make small coordinated joint changes that keep the outlet near the same point above the opening. Abrupt acceleration, reversal or repeated tilt oscillation can create a surge or slosh. Aim for a nearly stationary outlet and a narrow, steady stream landing inside the cup with margin from the rim.

Use successive head and wrist RGB observations to track the actual exit direction, airborne path and cup entry. Liquid already airborne continues along its old path after the bottle moves, while liquid still in the neck may keep flowing after tilt is reduced. Evaluate a correction only after newly emitted liquid is visible; chasing old drops with another correction can cause overshoot. Waiting for an API response or reviewing an old frame does not advance simulated drainage: request short native hold steps to observe the response at fixed pose. Keep hidden liquid positions and velocities out of this judgment.

Remember the last observed non-flowing orientation. Approach first flow with small joint changes spread over several allowed native steps, keeping the outlet above the opening; shorten each observation interval near onset. After an increment, hold the achieved pose for one or two native steps and inspect the outlet, cup interior and surrounding table. Flow can start after a delay at fixed tilt. Keep the achieved angle while waiting for this response instead of increasing tilt because the first frame has no stream. Establish a small flow entering the cup before increasing drainage. If the stream already enters cleanly, preserve that pose while it drains; a larger tilt is not automatically better.

If the new stream approaches the rim or fresh spill appears, promptly reduce tilt toward the remembered non-flowing orientation in a short controlled motion while keeping the outlet over the cup. Avoid a fast withdrawal or a broad rotation that sends the remaining neck flow across the table. Hold over the cup until fresh outflow stops and residual drops have landed. Then correct the observed miss direction with a small position change at fixed tilt. Re-establish low flow and observe its new landing before sustaining it. Change one cause at a time—outlet position or tilt—so the observed response can guide the next action; preserve cup stability throughout.

When successive observations show cup entry and no growing spill, maintain the pouring pose for short observed intervals and inspect the bottle for remaining liquid. If flow dwindles with liquid remaining, add only a small tilt increment while compensating the outlet arc, then hold and recheck entry. Drain a residual pool in the body, shoulder or neck the same way, without shaking the bottle. Liquid already visible in the cup or elapsed pouring time does not establish an empty bottle. Evaluate stopped flow together with the actual outlet orientation using the criteria below.

When the contents are visible, inspect the liquid-holding body, shoulder and neck for residual pools, together with the end of sustained flow at a draining orientation. Obtain another permitted RGB view when the gripper or bottle angle hides the relevant region. A visible residual pool requires further draining even when no stream is currently visible.

For an opaque bottle, use the accepted final drainage criterion: when the actual bottle outlet is visibly vertically downward and no liquid is flowing out of the visible outlet, judge the bottle empty indirectly and proceed to the final upright return. This criterion does not require another tilt adjustment or a previously observed stream. Assess the bottle body and outlet, accounting for any rotation within the grasp; wrist angle alone does not establish the outlet direction. An occluded outlet or uncertain orientation leaves the assessment unresolved.

At another effective draining orientation, the previously accepted indirect route also applies: observe outlet flow ending, continue short observations and make a small safe tilt adjustment with the outlet directed into the cup. If the outlet and landing region remain visible and no renewed flow appears across those checks, an indirect empty-bottle judgment is supported.

Record whether the assessment is direct, indirect or unresolved, with the supporting same-episode observations. For the vertically downward outlet criterion, explicitly state: "Judged empty indirectly: the visible bottle outlet was vertically downward with no liquid flowing out; the contents were not directly inspected." For the other accepted drainage route, describe its observed checks and likewise label the conclusion indirect. If flow resumes, continue draining and reassess. If the outlet or effective draining pose remains uncertain, obtain a permitted view or keep the assessment unresolved. Do not read hidden liquid volume or substitute an unobserved quantity for this evidence.

After direct confirmation or sufficient indirect drainage evidence, rotate upright smoothly while keeping the outlet above the cup for any last drops. Observe a short native hold there, then withdraw through clear space and check native completion. Include the indirect basis when reporting that assessment. If completion remains false, report the observed state and remaining uncertainty rather than declaring success. Repeated near-inversions cannot recover liquid already lost on the table.
